Independent investigations into marine cargo matters

01The Cargo

What moved, and what it was

From adhesives to zirconium ore, every cargo carries its own risks and its own questions when something goes wrong. Our work covers the cargo, how it moved, what happened and what can be learned from it.

Dry bulk and break bulk are among the oldest forms of commercial shipping. From ores, minerals and fertilisers to steel and other finished products, these cargoes underpin global trade. Their transport may expose them to hazards involving stowage, securing, handling, shifting, liquefaction, contamination, moisture, climatic conditions and, in some circumstances, fire or explosion.

02The Condition

The condition recorded, and the condition found

A cargo is described before it moves and described again when it arrives. Where those two descriptions differ, the difference is where the investigation begins.

Condition is a matter of record long before it becomes a matter of dispute. Certificates, tally sheets, photographs, temperature logs and survey reports each state something about a cargo at a particular moment. Our work is to establish what those records actually show, whether the condition they describe could have arisen in the manner claimed, and whether the damage or deterioration found on arrival is consistent with the conditions the cargo passed through.

03The Chain

Where responsibility passed, and when

Between the seller and the receiver a cargo passes through many hands. Responsibility passes with it, and rarely at the moment the parties assume.

A shipment is carried under contracts that allocate risk between the parties, and it is handled along the way by terminals, stevedores, hauliers and warehouse keepers who are not party to all of them. Establishing where responsibility sat at the moment a loss occurred requires the movement to be reconstructed against the documents that governed it, so that the point of transfer can be identified rather than assumed.

04The Loss

What was lost, and by which method it was measured

A shortage is a figure produced by a method. Before the figure can be relied upon, the method has to be examined.

Quantity is established by draft survey, by shore installation, by tally or by weighbridge, and each method carries its own tolerances and its own points of failure. Where two figures disagree, the disagreement often lies in the measurement rather than in the cargo. We examine how each figure was arrived at, what the method could and could not detect, and what quantity the evidence actually supports.

05The Risk

What can be learned once the facts are settled

Every matter that is properly investigated leaves behind something that can be used before the next shipment moves.

Once the facts of a loss are settled, the same evidence usually shows how the loss became possible in the first place. Recurring patterns in stowage, in documentation, in handling or in the choice of measurement method can be identified and addressed. The practice is engaged both after a loss and, where parties prefer, before one, to examine an intended shipment while the arrangements can still be changed.

07About

Independent and methodical

Cargo and transport matters rarely exist in isolation. They often involve commercial pressure, multiple interests and events that have developed over time. Understanding what happened requires more than a review of documents alone. It requires careful analysis of the events, circumstances and evidence behind them.

Our approach is independent and methodical. We examine the available information, identify what is known and what remains uncertain, and reconstruct the relevant events to establish the factual position. The objective is not to support a particular position, but to provide an assessment grounded in the evidence.

We are engaged by cargo insurers, commodity traders, shipping companies and legal parties when a shipment has gone wrong and the cause is unclear.

Since 1990, J.C. van Sas & Associates has provided independent technical assessment in cargo and transport matters. The practice combines more than three decades of practical experience, technical knowledge and a careful investigative approach to bring clarity to complex situations.

J.C. van Sas & Associates has long served as the Rotterdam representative of Martin Ottaway, a U.S.-based marine consultancy and engineering practice, just as Martin Ottaway has served as our representative in the United States. This reciprocal relationship provides access to additional maritime engineering expertise and international experience where required.
